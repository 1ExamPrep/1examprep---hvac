Exam Details

The Kentucky 595 Master HVAC exam is an ICC Contractor/Trades exam delivered by computer-based testing. Kentucky’s Contractor/Trades bulletin lists the 595 exam as 100 multiple-choice questions with an open-book format and a 4-hour time limit. The bulletin also lists an exam fee of $100 for the computer-based test (CBT).

Content is organized into major subject areas with assigned weights. The outline emphasizes a wide spread of HVAC competencies, including:

  • HVAC Principles, Terminology, Administration, and Safety
  • HVAC Installation Requirements
  • Venting, Duct, and Combustion Air
  • Gas Piping
  • HVAC Electrical
  • Boilers, Exhaust Systems, and Fire Protection
  • Plan Analysis

Important licensure note: Kentucky’s exam bulletin states that candidates are required to pass two examinations for licensure—one covering Business and Law and one covering technical HVAC knowledge and codes. This Ultimate package is designed to support that full-picture goal by including both technical references and the Kentucky business/law manual.

Open Book Test

The 595 exam is listed as open book. In practice, open book means you must be able to locate the correct requirement quickly and apply it accurately. The exam clock moves fast, and many questions are built so that you could find the answer in the book—but only if you already know where to go and how to confirm the rule without getting stuck.

To prepare effectively for an open-book HVAC exam, focus on three performance skills:

  • Navigation speed: Knowing which book to open first and where the likely chapter/section lives.
  • Precision reading: Catching the qualifiers that change the requirement (equipment type, installation conditions, venting details, clearances, locations, fuel type, and system configuration).
  • Decision-making under time: Knowing when a question needs a lookup versus when your first informed answer is the right call so you can keep pace across 100 questions.

This package supports those skills by keeping your preparation rooted in the references and building a repeatable method you can trust on exam day.

Licensing Steps

Kentucky licensing is administered by the Department of Housing, Buildings and Construction (DHBC) through the Licensing Branch and Division of HVAC. A straightforward path many applicants follow looks like this:

  1. Prepare for both required exams: Complete the Business and Law requirement and the technical HVAC/codes requirement tied to the ICC 595 Master HVAC exam.
  2. Pass your exam(s) and keep documentation: The Kentucky Master HVAC Contractor application includes an option to submit an Examination Score Certificate with your packet.
  3. Gather proof of experience: Kentucky’s Master HVAC Contractor application requires proof of experience and lists examples of acceptable documentation.
  4. Secure required insurance documentation: The application requires proof of general liability insurance and specifies minimum coverage amounts.
  5. Complete and submit the Master HVAC Contractor License Application: Include the required photo, fee, and supporting documents. Kentucky notes that the initial license is pro-rated based on birth month.
  6. Renew on schedule: Kentucky’s renewal process includes proof of continuing education and updated insurance documentation for Master HVAC Contractors.

State Requirements

Kentucky’s Master HVAC Contractor License Application outlines several key requirements that applicants should be ready to meet:

  • Score certificate or reciprocity documentation: The application includes checkboxes for submitting an Examination Score Certificate or, if applying by reciprocity, providing a letter of good standing from the state licensing agency/board.
  • Proof of insurance (required): Kentucky requires proof of general liability insurance with minimum coverage of $500,000 and property damage coverage of at least $300,000. The application specifies that the DHBC must be listed as the certificate holder and notes the license cannot be issued without a certificate on file.
  • Passport-sized photo: A recent passport-sized, color photograph taken within the last six months is required.
  • Proof of experience: The application states that proof of experience is required and provides examples such as W-2s, an affidavit from a Kentucky Master HVAC license holder, a copy of an out-of-state master HVAC contractor license or equivalent, or a Department of Defense Form DD 214, among other documentation.
  • Initial license is pro-rated: The application explains that the Department will issue an initial license for no less than seven (7) months and no more than eighteen (18) months, based on your birth month. It also includes a fee chart and indicates the fee is based on your birth month and the month you apply.

Renewal requirements: Kentucky’s licensing renewal application states that renewals must include proof of completing continuing education requirements and proof of General Liability Insurance and Workers Compensation Insurance (for Master HVAC Contractors and other contractor license types), submitted in the form of a certificate listing the Department as certificate holder. The renewal fee schedule lists Master HVAC Contractor renewal at $250, with a $125 late fee and $250 reinstatement when applicable.

Test Information and Study Materials

Master-level HVAC testing is rarely about one isolated fact. Most questions are written like jobsite decisions: a scenario is described, a condition is introduced, and you have to select the correct requirement or best answer based on codes, safety rules, and technical knowledge. Because the exam is open book and timed, the best prep includes both content review and performance training.

Build a “three-book decision” habit. Since your prep materials cover mechanical code, fuel gas code, and refrigeration/AC technology, train yourself to quickly decide which reference is the best starting point:

  • Mechanical code first for equipment installation rules, system requirements, clearances, access, duct and exhaust concepts, and code-driven scenario questions.
  • Fuel gas code first for gas piping, combustion air, venting logic, and questions tied to gas appliance installations.
  • Technical reference first when the question is less about a specific code section and more about HVAC system operation, components, measurements, diagnostics, or applied principles.

Practice what “Plan Analysis” really means. Plan analysis questions often test your ability to interpret what is shown, what is implied, and what must be confirmed by code or best practice. A strong approach is to slow down just enough to identify:

  • System type and fuel type
  • Occupancy/use and location conditions
  • Equipment category (and any special installation notes)
  • The single detail that controls the requirement (clearance, venting method, duct material, access, support, or sizing condition)

Train pacing for 100 questions. A 4-hour exam is long enough to cause fatigue, which is where avoidable mistakes happen. Build endurance into your prep by doing timed sets and increasing length over time. The goal is steady performance: accurate reading, efficient lookups, and consistent decision-making.

Create a “repeatable lookup routine.” When you do need to look something up, use a consistent pattern so you don’t lose minutes to wandering:

  • Step 1: Re-read the question and underline qualifiers (location, material, category, sizing, system type).
  • Step 2: Pick the reference that most directly controls the requirement.
  • Step 3: Navigate to the likely chapter/section and confirm you’re in the right neighborhood.
  • Step 4: Read the exact line that answers the question and confirm it matches the scenario.
  • Step 5: Answer and move on—then review your missed questions after your study session and learn the faster path for next time.

Make your missed questions valuable. Misses are where your score improves fastest. When you miss a question, record two things: (1) the correct requirement or concept, and (2) the best path to find it quickly in the reference next time. Over time, that transforms “open book” from a stress point into an advantage.
