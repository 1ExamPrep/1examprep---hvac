### Exam Details

The Mississippi Residential HVAC contractor examination evaluates knowledge associated with residential heating, ventilation, air conditioning, refrigeration, mechanical systems, fuel-gas installations, duct systems, load calculations, oil-burning equipment, and construction safety.

Candidates should combine practical HVAC experience with repeated use of the listed references. Examination questions may require direct code lookup, interpretation of a table, use of a formula, review of equipment requirements, or application of technical knowledge to a residential heating or cooling condition.

*Manual J—Residential Load Calculations*, Reprinted 2016, Eighth Full Edition, supports preparation involving residential heating and cooling load calculations. Proper load calculation is essential for selecting equipment that matches the building’s actual requirements.

Load-calculation preparation may involve indoor and outdoor design conditions, building orientation, insulation, windows, doors, infiltration, ventilation, walls, ceilings, floors, duct gains, duct losses, sensible heat, latent heat, and room-by-room loads.

Candidates should understand that equipment selection should not be based only on floor area or replacement of an existing unit with equipment of the same nominal size. Building characteristics, climate conditions, air leakage, glazing, insulation, occupancy, and duct location can affect the calculated load.

The International Mechanical Code, 2021, is a primary code reference in this package. It supports preparation involving mechanical-system installation, ventilation, exhaust, duct systems, combustion air, appliances, equipment clearances, access, piping, chimneys, vents, refrigeration systems, and related safeguards.

Mechanical-code questions may involve equipment location, access, clearances, condensate disposal, ventilation, exhaust systems, duct construction, air distribution, appliance installation, combustion air, venting, refrigeration machinery, or system protection.

Candidates should become familiar with the organization of the International Mechanical Code and practice moving between chapters, definitions, tables, exceptions, referenced standards, and the index. A single question may require several connected provisions.

The Ductulator supports practical airflow and duct-sizing work. Candidates can use it to review relationships between airflow, duct dimensions, velocity, friction loss, and equivalent duct sizes.

Duct-sizing questions may require candidates to identify a required airflow, select a friction rate, evaluate a duct size, or compare round and rectangular duct dimensions. Efficient use of the Ductulator requires repeated hands-on practice before examination day.

*HVAC Duct Construction Standards—Metal and Flexible*, 2005, 3rd Edition, supports preparation involving duct materials, construction classifications, reinforcement, joints, seams, supports, hangers, fittings, flexible duct, and installation practices.

Residential duct systems must be constructed and supported to maintain airflow, limit leakage, resist damage, and remain serviceable. Candidates should understand how duct size, pressure, material, reinforcement, connection methods, and installation conditions affect construction requirements.

*Modern Refrigeration and Air Conditioning*, 22nd Edition, supports broad technical review of refrigeration cycles, air-conditioning systems, heating systems, electrical components, controls, refrigerants, compressors, condensers, evaporators, metering devices, service procedures, troubleshooting, and equipment operation.

Refrigeration questions may involve heat transfer, pressure-temperature relationships, superheat, subcooling, system components, refrigerant flow, charging, recovery, evacuation, leak detection, or system performance.

Air-conditioning preparation may involve comfort conditions, sensible and latent heat, airflow, humidity, psychrometric principles, equipment operation, condensate, filters, blowers, coils, controls, and system efficiency.

Heating topics may involve electric heat, heat pumps, furnaces, combustion, burners, heat exchangers, controls, venting, airflow, and safety devices. Candidates should understand how the components work together as a complete system.

The International Fuel Gas Code, 2021, supports preparation involving fuel-gas piping, appliance installation, combustion air, venting, chimneys, regulators, pressure, shutoff valves, sizing, testing, and safety requirements.

Fuel-gas questions may require candidates to size piping, identify acceptable materials, review appliance connections, determine combustion-air requirements, evaluate venting arrangements, or locate required clearances and safeguards.

Candidates should practice using the fuel-gas code tables and understand the information needed before selecting a pipe size. Gas type, developed length, connected load, pressure, allowable pressure drop, and table conditions can affect the result.

*Residential Oil Burners*, 3rd Edition, supports preparation involving oil-fired heating equipment, fuel delivery, burners, nozzles, combustion, ignition, controls, draft, venting, maintenance, adjustment, and troubleshooting.

Oil-burner questions may involve fuel supply, pump pressure, nozzle selection, combustion air, ignition, flame characteristics, draft, safety controls, cleaning, testing, or service procedures.

The Code of Federal Regulations, 29 CFR Part 1926, supports preparation involving federal construction-safety requirements. Residential HVAC contractors may need to review electrical hazards, ladders, scaffolds, personal protective equipment, tools, material handling, housekeeping, and general jobsite responsibilities.

HVAC work may involve rooftops, attics, crawl spaces, ladders, sharp sheet metal, electrical systems, pressurized equipment, combustion appliances, refrigerants, and heavy components. Candidates should connect written safety requirements with practical field conditions.

The eight references function as a coordinated technical library. A single residential HVAC question may involve a mechanical-code provision, a load calculation, duct sizing, fuel-gas requirements, refrigeration knowledge, and a safety responsibility.
